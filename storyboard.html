Target Spec · v4

BSC AI Career Coach

A chat-based tech career coaching assistant for Ascendency. The coach's 57 training questions are grouped into ten discussion areas; a conversation enters one area, stays inside it for as long as it's useful, then deliberately closes it before opening another — so the user works through one subject at a time instead of receiving scattered one-off answers.

This is a target spec, not a description of the deployed bot. Up to v3 this document recorded what the code already did. From v4 it also records what we want it to do — the area-based conversation model below is a design request, not shipped behaviour. Every section is tagged so you can tell which is which: build from the PROPOSED and CHANGED items, and treat LIVE as already working.

Overview · State

Area Lifecycle — Enter, Stay, Leave PROPOSED

This replaces the old two-state model (no-context → context-captured). The difference that matters: being inside an area is a state the bot holds and can leave, not just a tag on the last answer. A conversation should be able to work through salary properly, close it off, and then open mentorship as a fresh subject — with the coach aware that salary was already covered.

STATE A · Outside any area

Opening messages, or the moment just after an area was closed.

  • Greeting and out-of-scope handled as they are today
  • A message pointing clearly at one area enters it directly
  • A broad ask gets a narrowing question whose options are the areas themselves
  • Returning users are offered areas they haven't covered yet
STATE B · Inside an area

One area is open. This is where most of the conversation happens.

  • Answers are drawn from that area's knowledge and its questions only
  • Follow-ups are read as belonging to this area unless clearly not
  • The coach can offer a natural next facet from the same area
  • Facets covered are recorded, so nothing is repeated on return
  • An off-area question triggers the exit check rather than a silent jump
STATE C · Closing the area

An explicit hand-off, so a switch is never accidental.

  • Triggered by the user asking to move on, or an off-area question
  • Also offered by the coach once an area's main facets are covered
  • Briefly sums up what was covered before closing
  • Then offers the remaining areas — returning to State A
  • Re-entering a closed area resumes it rather than starting over
The gap to close: today the only stored context is career_topic — a single overwritable field with no memory of what was already discussed, and no concept of an area being open or closed. See ISSUE-008 and ISSUE-009.

Area Flow · Area 9

Inside Salary & Negotiation PROPOSED

What happens during STATE B · Inside an area. Every answer ends with a question, and that question hands off to another facet we already have an answer for — so the conversation is led rather than left open. This is the first of ten; the other nine follow the same shape once this one is agreed.

Read this before the diagrams: the graph is a coverage map, not a router. An earlier draft of this page specified deterministic facet-to-facet routing — S1 leads to S2, S2 leads to S3. That was wrong, and wrong in a way this project has been caught by before: every deterministic layer in this coach has been outgrown, from isBroadStartingAsk to the keyword pre-check that rule 5 replaced. A fifteen-node edge table is a keyword regex in better clothes, and it breaks the first time someone says something that sits between two facets.

What actually happens inside an area: for every user message, one classification call places them in a stage — or finds they are leaving. The stage decides which of Otema's answers are injected as few-shot grounding, and the model writes a fresh reply from that material. Structure comes from the stage; the words come from the model. This is the wordalisation pattern the coach already uses in loadFewShotExamples(), keyed on stage instead of topic — so it is a smaller change than a router, not a bigger one.

So the facets and edges below record what material each stage can draw on. They are never consulted to decide what to say.
Seven examples per reply: the three closest, plus four drawn at random from the area. CHANGED Selecting only the nearest matches couples the answers too tightly to the examples — retrieval by word overlap returns examples that resemble each other as much as they resemble the question, so the model hears one shape and gives it back. The random four widen the voice it is matching: it hears her across the whole area, blunt in places and softer in others, rather than echoing the nearest neighbour.

The two sets are labelled differently in the prompt, and that label is doing the real work. The close three are voice reference for this question. The random four are introduced as "OTHER ANSWERS OF YOURS, on different questions in this same area. These are NOT about what she asked and you must not answer from them… Take the range, leave the content." Without that, the model treats a far-off example as material and tries to work it in. Only the close three are recorded as covered — the random four were shown, not drawn on, and marking them used would burn through an area in two turns.

When nothing fits at all. Retrieval returns its full quota however badly the examples match. Measured against the salary pool, a well-matched question scores 4 against its best example; "should they cover relocation and visa fees" scores 1, and "what laptop should I buy" scores 0 — and all three previously produced the same confident-looking prompt. When the best match scores 1 or less the framing now changes outright: "Nothing here is close to what she just asked, so take ONLY the voice… Do not bend her question towards these answers because they are what you have."

No guaranteed Otema answer. CHANGED Every reply used to be given at least one of her real answers, spliced in if none had won a place on merit. It was pinning a slot: stage C holds exactly one real answer of hers, so the same one went into all four turns of the review-cycle conversation — including turns where it scored zero against what was asked. Two of the three close slots were fixed for the whole conversation, which no penalty value could dislodge. Her voice now arrives on merit, or in the random four. The consequence is worth naming: if none of hers wins a close slot, the chance one appears in the random draw is 100% in stage A, 75% in stage B and 33% in stage C — because stage C is fourteen drafted answers to one of hers. That is a gap in the material, not in the selection, and it is the argument for reviewing the drafted answers into real ones.

Repeat examples are allowed on purpose. A used example is down-weighted by 2.5, not removed, so the same answer can surface again in a later turn when it is clearly the best fit. The number was 1.5 and did nothing: the gap between the best example and the fifth-best is about 3, so in the review-cycle conversation the same three facets came back on three consecutive turns and the coach asked the same closing question four times. The penalty applies to the three nearest only — the random four are shuffled uniformly and a repeat among them costs nothing. Some repetition is right — a point worth making once is often worth returning to — and the guards against restating a reply verbatim sit further downstream, in dropRepeatedSentences() and the do-not-repeat-yourself rules.
What the mapping bought us. All 31 answers already end in a question, and several of those closers already point at another facet. Mapping them found the 16 replies with nothing behind them — which is why the drafted pool exists. That work stands: it is what tells you whether a stage can actually carry a conversation. It just isn't a route.

What Happens After the Question PROPOSED

The level below the facet graph. Each closing question is a decision point, and the user's reply decides what comes next — but a reply only leads somewhere if we have an answer waiting. Rows shaded amber are replies with nothing behind them: content that has to be written or asked of Otema before the flow works.

Two kinds of closing question, and only one of them branches. REFINING closers gather detail to give a better version of the same answer — "What role and location?" doesn't move anywhere, it sharpens where you already are. BRANCHING closers genuinely fork the conversation, and those are the ones that need an answer per branch. Mistaking one for the other is how you end up with a question that leads nowhere.

OTEMA

How do I find out what salary I should be earning in my current or target tech role?

Job boards give you a rough range, but direct conversations with peers or mentors in similar roles are often your most accurate signal, since public salary data is still thin in many African markets. Also check whether a role pays local rates or globally-benchmarked remote rates — the gap can be large. What role and location?

Treat it as a normal, expected part of the process, not a confrontation — ask what range was set aside for the role, then ask for a day to think it over before responding. Do your research on the market rate first. Do you have a range in mind?

OTEMA

Document your concrete impact ahead of time, research what your role pays elsewhere, and schedule a dedicated conversation rather than raising it casually. Bring facts, not just a general sense that you deserve more. What's prompting this?

Signing bonus, equity, a learning budget, equipment, or even your title — titles affect your future earning trajectory more than people expect. For remote African-based roles, also check whether pay is in local currency or USD, given currency volatility. Which matters most to you?

G5

Freelance & contract rates

DRAFTED

How do I set my rate as a freelancer or contractor?

Work out what you need to earn in a year, then divide by realistic billable days — not 250, more like 150 once you account for finding the work and doing your own admin. That gives you a floor, not a target. Then check what the client's market pays, because an agency in Lagos and a client in Berlin are two different rate cards for identical work. Undercharging at the start is very hard to walk back with the same client. Who's your first client likely to be?

G6

Equity instead of salary

DRAFTED

A startup offered me equity instead of a higher salary. How do I know what it's worth?

Ask three things before you value it at anything: what percentage of the whole company is it, what's the vesting schedule, and has anyone actually sold shares yet. If they won't answer the first one, treat the equity as zero and negotiate on cash. I've watched too many people take a real pay cut for paper that never converted, especially at companies with no clear path to raising again. What stage is this company at?

DRAFTED

Inside Confidence & Imposter Syndrome PROPOSED

One classification call per message places the user in one of two stages; the stage scopes which of Otema's 5 real answers, plus 16 drafted facets, the model is shown; the model writes the reply from that material. Not a router — the edges recorded below are a coverage map read off closing questions, never consulted to decide what to say. Runs end-to-end in the local harness; not yet wired into the live edge function.

Two stages, not situational: what the self-doubt is doing, not what triggered it. A — an internal narrative being processed: comparing yourself to others, discounting your own wins, not feeling like you belong. B — self-doubt that has stalled a concrete action: not speaking up, not applying, not putting yourself forward. The distinguishing question is whether there's a decision sitting behind it. A is "I feel this way", B is "I'm not doing this because I feel this way" — and B is where the coach's job changes from processing a feeling to unsticking a choice.

The known risk with this split: a feeling and a stalled action can arrive in the same message with no explicit causal link between them — "I don't feel like I belong, and I also haven't applied for that promotion" doesn't say the feeling caused the inaction. The rule adopted is that a NAMED stalled action wins outright, even when the feeling is stated first and at length: stage A's own describes text says exactly this, so a long narrative that ends on one concrete thing not being done still classifies as B. Scenario 01 in the test sweep is built to probe this transition directly.
Seven examples per reply: the three closest, plus four drawn at random from the stage. Same mechanism as every other area, run here on this area's own numbers. Stage A holds 14 facets (3 real, 11 drafted); stage B holds 7 (2 real, 5 drafted). If none of Otema's real answers wins a close slot, the chance one still appears in the random four works out very differently between the two: in stage A, 3 real answers sit among the 11 left after the closest 3 are removed, so a random draw of 4 includes at least one of hers about 79% of the time. In stage B, removing the closest 3 from 7 leaves exactly 4 — the whole remainder, real answers included — so her voice is guaranteed to appear, not just likely to.

A used example is down-weighted by 2.5 rather than removed, so a facet can resurface in a later turn once it's clearly the best fit again — the same repetition allowance Salary's section explains in full.

Prompts · Routing

Routing Instructions

Routing system prompt bsc-coach.ts / botema-coach.ts — get instructions()
You are routing user messages to the correct [Persona] Career Coach function.

Current user profile: [career_stage, background, target_role, goals — or "No profile captured yet"]
Current career topic in focus: [topic or "none"]

ROUTING RULES — always call exactly one function, never respond directly:

1. OUT OF SCOPE — the message has nothing to do with TECH career coaching specifically: general trivia, unrelated technical help (e.g. "write me a script", "what's the capital of France"), creative writing requests, explicitly wanting a career/field/job that is NOT tech (e.g. "a field unrelated to tech", "I don't want to work in tech"), or anything else unrelated to tech careers, jobs, skills, mentorship, or mindset → call answerOutOfScope. Being about careers/jobs in general is not enough to be in scope — it has to be about a TECH career.

2. MINDSET — user expresses imposter syndrome, self-doubt, fear, anxiety, burnout, motivation loss, feeling they don't belong → call addressMindsetChallenge.

3. BACKGROUND — user explicitly shares detailed personal info: their current job title, years of experience, specific goals, location, or education level → call captureUserBackground. Do NOT use this for short replies like "I'm new" or "I'm a beginner".

4. GREETING — user says hello, hi, asks what you can do, or sends their very first message with no topic → call howCoachWorks.

5. NEEDS NARROWING — judge this from the message itself, not a fixed list: could you give ONE focused, specific answer right now, or would answering mean covering several genuinely different angles just to be safe? If it's the latter, call inviteUserContext to ask ONE short question narrowing down which angle to focus on, instead of covering all of them at once. This applies whether the message has no topic at all ("help me learn tech", "how do I start a career in tech") OR names a topic that still spans multiple distinct angles (e.g. "job search strategy" could mean CV, networking, the no-experience path, LinkedIn, or interview prep — which one? "what resources should I use" could mean web, data, or IT — which one?). A reliable self-check: if the answer you're about to write would naturally include branching phrasing like "if you're interested in X, do A — for Y, try B, and for Z, try C", that is proof the question was still broad. Stop and ask which ONE they want FIRST instead of writing that answer. When genuinely in doubt, prefer narrowing over answering — it only costs the user one tap, and prevents a long, multi-track answer covering options they didn't ask for. Skip narrowing only when the message already points to one specific, answerable facet ("how do I learn Python", "CV help", "salary negotiation"), or if the profile or conversation history already makes the intent clear. When you call inviteUserContext, always fill in its "question" and "options" arguments yourself — a short question and 3-5 answer options tailored specifically to what THIS user asked, not generic ones.

6. TOPIC/QUESTION (DEFAULT) — the message already points to one specific, answerable facet — a skill, a concrete question, a clearly single-angle request, even short ones like "I'm new to tech" or "I want to be a developer" → call updateCareerTopic with the best topic you can infer. The answer that follows should stay tightly focused on that one facet — don't pull in every related sub-topic just because they live in the same knowledge area. Keep it short: a few lines, not a comprehensive rundown of everything related.

When in doubt between updateCareerTopic and answerOutOfScope, prefer updateCareerTopic unless the message is clearly unrelated to tech careers.

Always call exactly one function.
Prompts · Safety Net

The Narrowing Self-Check

NARROW_SELF_CHECK converser.ts — exported constant
Self-check before you answer — read this carefully: if your answer would name more than one distinct direction/track/option side by side (e.g. "for web development, do A; for data, do B; for IT, do C"), that is ALWAYS wrong — even if you also end with a clarifying question. Listing every track "to be safe" instead of asking which one FIRST is exactly the mistake to avoid; ending with a question does not excuse it. The moment you notice yourself about to name a second distinct track/option in the same answer, stop — do not send that answer. Output ONLY these two lines instead, exactly, and nothing else:
NARROW_QUESTION: <a short question tailored to what they asked>
NARROW_OPTIONS: <option 1> | <option 2> | <option 3>
(3-5 short options tailored to their message, each under 6 words, separated by " | ")
NO_INVENTED_FIGURES converser.ts — added 15 Aug, after the invented-salary incident
You have no live pay data and no web access, so never state a specific salary, rate or currency figure, and never claim to be citing market data, surveys or research — you have none. If someone asks what a role pays, tell them how to find out instead: what to compare, who to ask, and what makes a source trustworthy. Being honest that reliable public data is thin in many markets is a better answer than a confident number.
LONG_FORM_ESCAPE_HATCH converser.ts — exported constant
If — and only if — the user explicitly asked for something that genuinely needs a longer, multi-paragraph answer (a full draft, a full rewrite, or they asked you to explain more or go deeper), start your reply with the exact marker LONG_FORM_OK alone on its own first line, then continue with your full answer as normal on the following lines. Do not include this marker for an ordinary short answer — it should be rare, not the default.

Read the "enforced by" column before trusting any row. A rule enforced by prompt is a request the model usually honours. A rule enforced by code is one it demonstrably would not honour, so the output is edited after generation. Treating the two alike is how you end up relying on a rule that has failed seven times.

The pattern, stated once: when an instruction fails twice, write the check instead of rewording it a third time.
